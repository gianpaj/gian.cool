# Fax history: non-Wikipedia sources

Retrieved 2026-11-03. This file collects what sources outside Wikipedia add to the baseline in `wikipedia/fax.md`. Quotes are exact. Media downloads are credited in `../images/web/CREDITS.md` and `../audio/CREDITS.md`.

Blocked to scripted access, so not read directly: Library of Congress, NOAA Digital Library, Smithsonian NMAH object pages, Becker's, Fierce Healthcare, NJBIZ, and the Japanese Patent Office page. Some of their items appear here through search-index metadata and are marked as such.

## 1. Jonathan Coopersmith, *Faxed* (Johns Hopkins UP, 2015)

**"Fax Machines", Jonathan Coopersmith, ETHW (IEEE STARS article), last edited 14 Jun 2018**
https://ethw.org/Fax_Machines
- This is the book's argument in short form, written by Coopersmith himself.
- Bain: British patent 9745, 27 May 1843; US patent 5957, 5 Dec 1848. Commercial service in the 1860s, first profitable service after WWI, sustained business-fax efforts from the mid-1960s, and essential status in the 1980s, "first in Japan".
- By 2000 email had made fax "obsolescent, though not obsolete."
- Rivalries by era: Bain vs Bakewell (1840s), Caselli vs Meyer (1860s), Korn vs Belin (1910s), Wirephoto vs Soundphoto (1930s), Times Facsimile vs Radio Inventions Inc. (post-WWII), Western Union vs Xerox (1950s–60s).
- Over-optimism labels: a "coming wonder" (1930s), "Cinderella" (1940s), "commercial infant" (1950s), "sleeping giant" (1960s). Promoters kept saying "This time it will be different."
- Why it took so long: cost, speed, and quality goals barely changed while rising expectations outran capability. Synchronization and line noise forced resends, electrochemical paper kept only days or weeks once moistened, and early success came only in niches (newspapers, weather, military).
- Korn: Berlin–Nuremberg photo in 42 min (1904); newspapers in Germany, France, and England published photos sent in 12 min (1907). By 1928 at least 19 systems were in development, 4 of them commercial; about 100 picture machines existed in 1933.
- Western Union Desk-Fax: 1949 prototype in 8 cities, "over 40,000 machines" in the 1950s, "tens of thousands" made into the 1960s. No phone-line fax matched that volume until the 1970s.
- Xerox LDX (1964): about 1,100 lb, leased for $800/month, 8 pages/min.
- Deregulation: US gradual from 1968, Japan rapid in 1972. Page times: G1 (1968) 6 min, G2 (1976) 3 min, G3 (1980) 1 min.
- Japan drove the boom through demand from an "ideographic language" and supply from consumer-electronics makers. Sharp FO-2000 "HAYAX" (1981): 70 kg, 175 W, A4 in 20 s at 9600 bps, about ¥3 million.
- US installed base: 25,000 (1970), 250,000 (1980), 500,000 (1985), 5 million (1990), "nearly all made in Japan".
- FedEx ZapMail lost over $300 million in 1984–86.
- PCs grew from 130 million (1991) to 775 million (2004). Stand-alone fax sales peaked in the US in 1997 and in Japan in 2000.
- Further reading cited: Coopersmith, "Facsimile's False Starts", *IEEE Spectrum*, Feb 1993, 46–49; Coopersmith, "Creating Fax Standards: Technology Red in Tooth and Claw?", *Kagakugijutsushi* 11 (2010); Wakabayashi, "Research and Events that Permitted Facsimile Use to Explode in Japan", IEEE GLOBECOM Workshops 2009.
- Reliability: author's own summary, well referenced. The page contradicts itself on a few dates (see last section).

**Review of *Faxed*, Hyungsub Choi (Seoul National Univ.), *Shashi* 4(1), 2016, pp. 27–29, CC BY 3.0 US**
https://shashi.pitt.edu/ojs/shashi/article/view/33/129 (DOI 10.5195/shashi.2016.33)
- The book frames two questions: why fax took so long to go mainstream, and how it persisted once digital tech arrived. It spans the 1842 Bain "Electro-Chemical Copying Telegraph" patent to the early 21st century.
- Coopersmith's p. 49 cost table shows early fax was "prohibitively expensive yet technologically premature".
- Japan: in the mid-1920s Mainichi Shimbun approached US and German engineers, then Nippon Electric built a domestic Tokyo–Osaka system within a year. Coopersmith calls this "exemplif[ying] the Japanese approach to modernization" (p. 47).
- Why Japan: kanji (p. 138), the non-sequential address system that made people fax maps to visitors (p. 168), and hanko seals.
- The government folded fax into its early-1970s "information society (jōhōka shakai)" vision (p. 139), coordinated the domestic standard, and leveraged it into G3.
- The book carries 1,148 footnotes.
- Reliability: scholarly review with page cites into the book.

**"The Rise And Fall Of The Fax Machine", Texas A&M Today, 6 Aug 2015**
https://stories.tamu.edu/news/2015/08/06/the-rise-and-fall-of-the-fax-machine/
- "Until the 1970s, it was about 10 to 100 times more expensive to send a fax than just sending something by U.S. mail."
- "It was a great idea, but it sort of bounced around for several decades without much success."
- Three obstacles: slow speed, high prices, incompatible machines. First profitable use was AT&T/AP Wirephoto in 1935.
- "By the early 1980s, the technology had improved so that you could transmit one page in about 20 seconds."
- "In a virtuous cycle for consumers, Japanese manufacturers viciously competed against each other", while still cooperating on compatibility.
- "Email just changed everything."
- Reliability: university press release with direct quotes.

**"The history of the fax machine (and why it's not dead yet)", Joey Watson & Ian Coombe, ABC RN *Late Night Live*, 28 Mar 2019**
https://www.abc.net.au/news/2019-03-28/history-of-the-fax-machine-why-it-is-still-alive/10930232
- Kanji's tens of thousands of characters pushed businesses toward sending handwriting.
- "By the 1980s, Japan was the leading market for fax machines."
- "Up until 1980, one of the big problems of fax machines is that companies were creating incompatible machines." In 1980 the ITU adopted "a Japanese-based standard called G3".
- "Faxing encouraged people to expect instant electronic communications."
- A 2017 survey of 200 large firms found 82% sent at least as many faxes as in 2016.
- Reliability: broadcaster interview; the survey is not named.

**"Reading Room: Jonathan Coopersmith '78", Tara Thean, *Princeton Alumni Weekly*, 8 Jul 2015**
https://paw.princeton.edu/article/reading-room-jonathan-coopersmith-78
- Until the 1960s a fax was "100 times more costly than sending a letter". Telegram and telex were the main rivals.
- "It was a very slow curve to get to the point of success."
- Fax-on-demand example: fax a number to get ski conditions.
- Reliability: interview.

**"Why do we still have fax machines?", Eleanor Cummins, *Popular Science*, 3 May 2018**
https://www.popsci.com/why-fax-machines-still-exist/
- Coopersmith: first commercial service in France in 1863. Half a century to profitability, then another half to become worldwide. Early high-quality fax cost "one or two orders of magnitude" more than alternatives.
- "Faxing, especially, is a network technology… the more people who use it, the more valuable it becomes."
- US sales peaked at 3.6 million in 1997 (citing NYT, 2005).
- Reliability: magazine feature with quotes.

**Talk: "Forging the Fax: How Fax Machines Helped Create 'Alternative Facts'", Coopersmith, UT Austin, 10 Feb 2017**
https://sites.utexas.edu/hps/jonathan-coopersmith-10-feb-2017-forging-the-fax/
- Junk fax and fax-borne misinformation prefigure spam. Coopersmith proposes a communications Gresham's law: "bad pseudo-data drives out good information."
- *Faxed* co-won the 2016 Business History Conference Hagley Prize.
- Reliability: talk abstract.

**Other Coopersmith interviews:**
- Marginal Revolution, 9 Jun 2012, citing the *Washington Post*: https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2012/06/peak-fax-japan-fact-of-the-day.html
  - About 59% of Japanese households had a fax (Cabinet Office, March 2012), against about 3% of US homes in the early 1990s.
  - Coopersmith: "I don't think any other nation comes close to Japan."
- Not found: any Coopersmith source naming MITI. The IEEE milestone below credits the Ministry of Posts and Telecommunications, plus NTT and KDD.

## 2. IEEE / ETHW and Japanese engineering bodies

**IEEE Milestone: "International Standardization of G3 Facsimile, 1980", dedicated 5 Apr 2012, NTT Yokosuka R&D**
https://ethw.org/Milestones:International_Standardization_of_G3_Facsimile,_1980
- Plaque: "This site commemorates the creation of the Modified READ two-dimensional coding for G3 facsimile developed through the careful collaboration of NTT and KDDI. … This innovative and efficient standard enabled the worldwide commercial success of facsimile."
- Japan's driving force was the Ministry of Posts and Telecommunications.
- G1 (1968) was little used. G2 (1976) was "the so-called 'three minute facsimile'". G3 was the "one-minute facsimile".
- MR offered "twice the compression performance" of MH.
- At the CCITT Kyoto meeting, the Japanese delegates offered the MR patents free of charge if MR was adopted.
- NTT launched the F-Net fax network in 1981. MMR followed as T.6 in 1984.
- Primary refs listed: Yamazaki et al., "Quick-FAX" (1976); Yamada, "Edge-Difference Coding", *IEEE Trans. Commun.* (Aug 1979); Yasuda et al., "Advances in FAX", *Proc. IEEE* (Apr 1985).
- Reliability: official IEEE milestone text, written by the nominators (NTT side).

**NE-type phototelegraph (Niwa & Kobayashi, NEC, 1928). This is an IEEJ honour, not an IEEE milestone.**
- I found no IEEE milestone for it; I checked all 315 pages in ETHW "Category:Milestones".
- Akihiko Yamada, "5th IEEJ Commemoration: One Step on Electro-Technology", May 2012: https://history.computer.org/pubs/2012-05-yamada-5th-IEEJ-Commemoration.pdf
  - IEEJ honoured "the NE-type phototelegraphic system (facsimile machine) (NEC)" on 22 Mar 2012.
  - It was "used to transmit pictures for a newspaper company on the occasion of Emperor Hirohito's enthronement ceremony held in 1928".
  - Niwa later became the first president of Tokyo Denki University.
- IEEJ object sheet (Japanese), https://www.iee.jp/file/foundation/data02/ishi-05/ishi-0405.pdf, per search-index summary; my own text extraction was garbled:
  - The machine is shown at the National Museum of Nature and Science, Ueno.
  - It was used in Kyoto after the imported Belin system performed poorly.
  - Public Tokyo–Osaka photo service started in 1930.
- NEC Stories (Japanese), Apr 2022: https://jpn.nec.com/corporateblog/202204/03.html
  - The enthronement took place in Kyoto in Nov 1928. Tokyo Nichi Nichi Shimbun (now Mainichi) tested foreign machines and found NEC's, completed in May 1928, best for clarity and speed; photos went Kyoto→Tokyo.
  - Niwa was 35 and NEC's technical department head. He set up a transmission section (伝送科) in 1927.
  - Niwa was named one of the JPO's Ten Great Inventors in 1985.
- Reliability: IEEJ is authoritative. NEC is the company telling its own story.

**Oral history: Hans Georg Musmann, interviewed by Frederik Nebeker, IEEE History Center, 30 Aug 1994**
https://ethw.org/Oral-History:Hans_Musmann
- Analog fax took "about fifteen minutes" per page in the 1960s. A page to the US cost "about twenty to thirty D-marks".
- "In the middle of the 70s—1975 or 1976—the standard for facsimile was fixed." Competing proposals came from IBM (US), KDD (Japan), and the German PTT with TU Braunschweig.
- "The proposal we presented was too complicated to be adopted." "So the result was a compromise, called a modified Huffman code." That code cut bits "only by a factor of four"; the 2-D second standard aimed at a factor of 10.
- "Thus facsimile became the first digital transmission of visual information, I think."
- "There was a special need for Japan; the Western countries already had Telex."
- Reliability: first-hand participant in CCITT standardization; recalled 18 years later.

**Other ETHW oral histories and articles:**
- William F. Schreiber, 6 Oct 1998: https://ethw.org/Oral-History:William_F._Schreiber
  - "Who knows today that facsimile is older than the telephone? But it is."
  - His MIT lab built the AP Laserphoto system and a scan converter between the US and foreign fax standards, installed at the AP's New York hub.
- Thomas Huang, 20 Mar 1997: https://ethw.org/Oral-History:Thomas_Huang
  - His 1960s 2-D binary-image coding influenced Japan's Modified READ.
  - "The main idea was there way back in the '60s, but not until about ten or fifteen years ago did the fax become popular."
- Paul Baran, 24 Oct 1999: https://ethw.org/Oral-History:Paul_Baran
  - Co-founded Interfax Inc. around 1989: computers that read incoming faxes, starting with magazine reader-response cards.
- "Telautograph" (IEEE-USA InSight, Feb 2018): https://ethw.org/Telautograph
  - Gray began in 1887; the Gray National Telautograph Co. formed in 1888. By 1890 different people's handwriting was distinguishable.
  - Longest link New York–Chicago (1893). Used for coastal-artillery fire control in the 1890s, in banks, hospitals, and steel mills, and at Grand Central Terminal until the 1960s.
  - Bought by Arden/Mayfair in 1971.
- "Western Union": https://ethw.org/Western_Union
  - "In 1948, Garvice Ridings developed a small, easy-to-operate low-cost desktop facsimile machine named Desk-Fax, and within a few years, 40,000 of these machines were in use."
- Reliability: IEEE History Center pages, lightly referenced.

## 4. Mid-century stories

**"Delivering The Fax", George Mannes, *Invention & Technology* 14(4), Spring 1999**
https://inventionandtech.com/node/86052
- W. G. H. Finch was born in Birmingham, England, in 1897. He engineered for Hearst's INS and spent a year as assistant chief engineer at the FCC before founding his own company in 1935. He held more than 200 patents.
- His late-1930s home receiver cost $79.50, or $49.95 as a kit. It was "limited by its glacial slowness": 11 min a page. Crosley sold it as the Reado from 1939.
- The St. Louis Post-Dispatch "Radio Edition" started 7 Dec 1938 via KSD, printed on 15 receivers.
- More than 20 US stations ran fax broadcasts in the 1930s–40s. Broadcasters Faximile Analysis formed in 1944 with about 20 broadcasters, led by John V. L. Hogan.
- The Miami Herald ran a fax edition for about a year from 1948 at about $2,000/month; the NYT ran fax bulletins in 1948.
- Finch's company went bankrupt by 1952.
- Reliability: well-edited history magazine.

**"A Look Back at the Radio Newspaper of the Air", John Schneider, *Radio World*, 19 Nov 2014**
https://www.radioworld.com/?p=36371
- Finch receivers cost $125 and used a 5-inch thermal roll ($1, about a week). A 12-inch page took about 20 min, synced to 60 Hz mains.
- FCC experiments in 1937–38 at KSTP, WHO, WGH, WOR, WGN, WHK, WSM, and WWJ. McClatchy's "Radio Bee" bought 100 receivers.
- RCA ran overnight service on KSD from 1937, then on UHF W9XZY (31.6 MHz, 100 W, about 20 mi). It supplied 15 receivers at $260.
- Crosley Reado: $60 and $80, overnight over WLW until 1942.
- Failure causes: cost, jams, static, two incompatible standards, wary advertisers; the public "had neither asked for, nor cared about, the technology." WWII paper shortages ended most stations.
- Reliability: radio-industry trade press, detailed.

**RCA Ultrafax, *Radio Age* (RCA house magazine), late 1948, via Lantern**
https://lantern.mediahist.org/catalog/radioageresearch194850newyrich_0019 and `_0020`
- Demonstrated at the Library of Congress in October 1948. The OCR reads "October 2L", so the day is uncertain.
- "a million words a minute". *Gone With the Wind*, 1,047 pages, went "in about two minutes" over 3 miles from NBC's WNBW tower.
- Built by RCA Labs "in cooperation with the Eastman Kodak Company" and NBC: a CRT flying spot scans a film strip, and the receiver records onto film.
- Sarnoff called it "as significant a milestone in communications as was the splitting of the atom in the world of energy". His handwritten first message: "May Ultrafax, as swift as light, open a new and useful service for mankind everywhere."
- Reliability: RCA's own publicity; poor OCR.

**"Radio-Facsimile for Telegram Delivery", C. Jelinek Jr. & K. R. Jones, *Western Union Technical Review*, Apr 1951, pp. 41–50 (AIEE paper, Jan 1951)**
https://worldradiohistory.com/Archive-Company-Publications/Western-Union/Western-Union-Technical-Revue-1951-04.pdf
- Experiment in Baltimore, 1947. The first Telecar was a 1946 Plymouth with a two-way radio-telephone and a fax recorder; FM at 152–162 MHz, 45 W.
- Delivery time "could be cut to about one-half"; 70–80 messages per 8-hour tour.
- In 1948 the FCC refused 152–162 MHz and assigned four channels at 35–44 MHz. The base station went up to 250 W and coverage grew from 28 to 75 sq mi.
- Each message took about 65 s, so one channel could serve at most 3 cars. About 4% of messages were rerun, a quarter of them because of noise.
- City-wide Baltimore service: 7 Telecars plus a spare, 4 fixed stations.
- Reliability: primary engineering paper by the developers.

**Xerox LDX and Magnafax Telecopier**
- LDX announced 5 May 1964 for "a microwave channel, coaxial cable, or special telephone lines". Rental: $550/month scanner, $650/month printer, $800 for both. Source: archived Xerox blog quoted in search results; the page now redirects.
- Phil Goldstein, *BizTech*, 27 Sep 2016: https://biztechmagazine.com/article/2016/09/how-xerox-magnafax-telecopier-helped-make-fax-verb
  - Magnafax (1966): 46 lb, about 6 min per letter page, any phone line.
  - Rent "at least $50 per month": $35 plus a $15 minimum per 100 pages. The *Life* ad said: "Mail letters over the phone!"
- Xerox patent US 3,149,201 (filed 1962, granted 1964) describes the LDX-type design: CRT flying-spot scanner, photomultiplier, and xerographic printer over "co-axial cables or microwave relay equipment".
- Reliability: BizTech is a trade site; the LDX prices come from a secondary snippet.

**Digital fax: SRI, Dacom, Ricoh**
- SRI, "75 Years of Innovation: Digital FAX machine": https://www.sri.com/story/75-years-of-innovation-digital-fax-machine/
  - In 1970 SRI was "commissioned by Savin, CBS Television Services and Dacom". Its Z-60 prototype sent at 4,800 bit/s.
  - Ricoh licensed the work in 1973: RIFAX 600S, about 60 s per A4 page. Tokyo–New York satellite demo in Apr 1973; rebranded Rapifax 100 in 1974.
- Ricoh corporate history: https://www.ricoh.com/about/history/1970/rifax600s
  - RIFAX 600S (1973) sent "an A4 standard page in one minute", against up to 6 min for analog. It used a 1,200-baud modem at 4,800 bps.
  - It was enabled by the 1972 revision of Japan's Public Telecommunications Act. NASA sent a thank-you letter in Sep 1975.
  - Ricoh says its procedure fed into T.30. Honours: IEEJ One Step (2016); Essential Historical Material for S&T No. 00170 (FY2014).
- RFC 803, "Dacom 450/500 Facsimile Data Transcoding", Agarwal, O'Connor & Mills, 2 Nov 1981: https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/rfc803
  - The Dacom 450 used a 2-D run-length code, "encoded two rows at a time". Detail mode was about 1726×2200 pels, sent in 585-bit frames (24-bit sync, 37-bit leader, 512 data bits, 12-bit CRC).
  - The Dacom 500 used T.4 at about 50 kbit/s on INTELPOST.
- Dacom patent US 4,135,214 (D. R. Weber, filed 2 Jul 1969): adaptive run-length coding for "business letters, weather maps, newspapers".
- Reliability: SRI and Ricoh are claimant histories; the RFC is primary.

**Exxon Qwip and GammaFax**
- Qwip: see The Henry Ford entry in §3.
  - A 1985 Data Decisions buyer's guide lists Qwip models 1200/1300/2000/2150/2310: http://bitsavers.org/pdf/dataDecisions/1985_Communications_Systems/Vol2_940.pdf (seen in search results, not opened).

  - "industry's first PC/fax board and software package that came to market in 1985".
  - GammaLink merged into Dialogic in 1994 (secondary).
- Reliability: museum record; the alumni citation is honorific.

## 5. Politics, culture, persistence

**"News Trickles Back to China Via Student Faxes", Laurent Belsie, *Christian Science Monitor*, 12 Jun 1989**
https://www.csmonitor.com/1989/0612/ademo.html
- A core group of 20 Caltech students faxed Western news reports to businesses in China. Dali Ding: "We can fax back pictures and that is probably more convincing than just oral reports."
- The Chinese Alliance for Democracy ran 10 fax machines in San Francisco, LA, Houston, Boston, Tokyo, Hong Kong, and South America.
- More than $200,000 was raised in New York. One contact's fax machine was confiscated.
- Follow-up, CSM 29 Sep 1989 (https://www.csmonitor.com/1989/0929/dchin.html): authorities "started taking away fax machines."
- Hong Kong daily fax digest: https://qz.com/1688553/how-a-hong-kong-fax-machine-informed-millions-of-chinese-in-1989 (not opened).
- Reliability: contemporaneous reporting.

**1991 Soviet coup**
- UPI, "Coup alert came by fax", 20 Aug 1991: https://www.upi.com/Archives/1991/08/20/Coup-alert-came-by-fax/6233004534291/
  - The first fax, timed 10:54 p.m. EDT on Sunday, came from a Yeltsin-government official to Allen Weinstein's Center for Democracy in Washington: "It is Military Coup. Tanks are everywhere." It arrived before the first news reports, around 11:30 p.m.
  - Weinstein: "They (Soviet leaders) tried to shut down the faxes, but we're still getting them."
- Laurent Belsie, "Technology Thwarted Coup Leaders' Success", CSM, 26 Aug 1991: https://www.csmonitor.com/1991/0826/26091.html
  - Faxes, email, bulletin boards, and cable TV "opened the floodgates of information". The Interfax joint venture spread news abroad.
- Reliability: contemporaneous wire and newspaper reports.

**The late-1980s US fax craze**
- Daniel B. Wood, "Facsimilie Transmissions Proliferate" (sic), CSM, 13 Dec 1989: https://www.csmonitor.com/1989/1213/ffax.html
  - Machines cost under $1,000, against $7,500–10,000 a decade earlier. A 20-page document cost $1.25 by fax and $14 by FedEx.
  - Dataquest: 157,000 units sold in 1985 ($500M) and 500,000 in 1987 (about $1B). NOPA: 424,600 (1987), 863,000 (1988), and a forecast of 2.3M for 1992.
  - McCue's public-fax directory grew from 411 entries (Aug 1986) to 9,000 (Jan 1989).
  - Uses included phone-order menus, prescriptions, homework, valentines, coin-op fax in Tokyo coffee shops, and Chinese dissidents. Junk fax caused a "furor over waste".
  - Tim Choy, a PR partner: "Within three months, we had forgotten what it was like before we had it."
- CSM, 27 Aug 1987, "Lower prices, greater versatility transmit 'faxes' to more offices": https://www.csmonitor.com/1987/0827/ffax.html
  - 191,000 sold in 1986, an installed base of 600,000, and 50,000 sold in 1983 (CAP International).
- Lunch by fax: a February 1989 report says the Lakewood Deli recovered the cost of its order-taking fax in under a month. It surfaced in a search snippet linked to NJBIZ (https://njbiz.com/whats-coming-next-in-fax-machines); the page returned 403, so this is unverified.
- Reliability: CSM is contemporaneous; the trade-group forecasts proved optimistic.

**Junk fax enforcement**
- FCC Enforcement Bureau, Fax.com forfeiture order, 5 Jan 2004: https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DOC-225128A1.pdf
  - $5,379,000 for 489 violations at $11,000 each. Proposed in Aug 2002, it was the largest TCPA fine the FCC had proposed and its first against a "fax broadcaster".
- Reliability: primary.

**Japan's 2021 de-faxing push**
- In April 2021 Taro Kono, the administrative reform minister, told Kasumigaseki ministries to switch to email. He called faxes a "hindrance" to telework and wanted them abolished "as a rule" by the end of June.
- More than 400 objections came back (reported by the Hokkaido Shimbun). Ministries cited confidential uses such as civil courts and police, and the plan was dropped.

## 6. Technical: the handshake and Group 3 coding

**ITU-T T.30 (09/2005), procedures for document facsimile** (primary)
https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-T.30
- §4.1.1 Answering: at least 0.2 s of silence, then CED, "a continuous 2100 Hz ± 15 Hz tone for a duration of not less than 2.6 s and not more than 4.0 s", then a 75 ± 20 ms gap. V.8-capable terminals send ANSam instead (§4.1.2).
- §4.2 CNG: "1100 Hz, ON for 0.5 second, OFF for 3 seconds" (±38 Hz, ±15%). Function: "To indicate a calling non-speech terminal."
- Control messages (DIS, DCS, CFR, MCF, …) are HDLC frames "at 300 bits per second ±0.01% utilizing the characteristics of V.21 channel No. 2". Each turnaround starts with a preamble, "a series of flag sequences for 1 s ± 15%".
- TCF (training check), sent at the chosen high-speed rate: "A series of 0 for 1.5 s ± 10%." The receiver answers CFR (X010 0001) to accept or FTT (X010 0010), "rejecting the training signal and requesting a retrain". That fall-back loop is the stepping-down sound on bad lines.
- ITU-T V.21 (11/1988): channel 2 has a nominal mean of 1750 Hz. Binary 1 (Z) = 1650 Hz and binary 0 (A) = 1850 Hz, so the DIS "warble" is the same 300-baud FSK that modems used for data.
- Decoded example: `../audio/fax-answering-tones.ogg` shows 2.5 s of 2100 Hz, then V.21 preamble plus DIS, then 3 s of silence, then the DIS repeats (table in `../audio/CREDITS.md`).

**ITU-T T.4 (07/2003), Group 3 terminal characteristics** (primary)
https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-T.4
- "1728 black and white picture elements along the standard scan line length of 215 mm ±1%".
- Every coded line starts with a white run; a line that begins black starts with white-run 0. Runs 0–63 use terminating codes; runs of 64–1728 use a make-up code plus a terminating code. Black and white have separate tables.
- EOL = `000000000001`, which also precedes the first line. RTC (end of page) = six EOLs.
- Minimum coded line time is 20 ms (options of 10, 5, 0, and 40 ms), padded with fill bits; maximum is under 13 s.
- Sample codes:

| Run length | White code | Black code |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 00110101 | 0000110111 |
| 1 | 000111 | 010 |
| 2 | 0111 | 11 |
| 3 | 1000 | 10 |
| 4 | 1011 | 011 |
| make-up 64 | 11011 | 0000001111 |
| make-up 1728 | 010011011 | 0000001100101 |

- Worked example, blank line: W1728 make-up `010011011` + W0 `00110101` + EOL = 29 bits instead of 1,728 raw bits (about 60×).
- At 9600 bit/s the 20 ms minimum is 192 bits, so a blank line is padded with fill bits and the real saving is about 9×. Text lines compress less.
- Worked example, a line with 100 white, 4 black, then 1624 white: make-up 64 (`11011`) + W36 (`00010101`), then B4 (`011`), then make-up 1600 (`010011010`) + W24 (`0101000`), then EOL. That is 44 bits for 1,728 pixels.
- Background: R. Hunter & A. H. Robinson, "International Digital Facsimile Coding Standards", *Proc. IEEE* 68(7), July 1980, 854–867. This is the standard citation; I did not read it.

## Where sources disagree with or extend Wikipedia

**Dates and firsts**
- **Bain patent date.** Wikipedia gives 27 May 1843; ETHW's reference list says "granted 27 November 1843". Bain's own US patent 5,957 settles it: the British patent was "dated the 27th day of May, 1843" and its specification "duly enrolled the 27th day of November". Both dates are right for different steps.
- **Caselli service start.** The Wikipedia Fax article says 1865. The Pantelegraph article says public use from Feb 1863. Coopersmith says 1863 in PopSci and the ETHW timeline, but 1865–67 in the ETHW text. A Paris–Le Havre telegram (c. 1867) and the 20-centimes-per-cm² tariff come from the Musée des arts et métiers.
- **Telautograph patent.** ETHW says Gray patented it in 1887; Wikipedia says 31 Jul 1888. NMAH lists patents 386,814/386,815, which fit 1888. Treat 1887 as the filing year.
- **Ranger photoradiogram.** Wikipedia: Coolidge photo sent New York→London on 29 Nov 1924. *Radio News* (Feb 1925): the first public demo was Sunday 30 Nov, with photos sent London (Marconi House)→Carnarvon→Riverhead→Broad Street, New York, and the Coolidge image captioned "as reproduced on the receiving apparatus". Tests probably ran both ways; check a primary RCA or NYT account before narrating the direction.
- **First digital fax.** Wikipedia credits the Dacom Rapidfax, late 1960s. SRI and Ricoh say the RIFAX 600S (1973), sold in the US as the Rapifax 100 (1974), was the first commercial digital fax, from SRI work commissioned in 1970 by Savin, CBS, and Dacom. Dacom's compression patent was filed in 1969. The product name is "Rapifax", not "Rapidfax".
- **GammaFax year.** Wikipedia and the UIUC citation say 1985. ETHW's text says the first PC fax board came in 1986, while its own timeline says 1985.
- **NE-type phototelegraph.** The Wikipedia Niwa article omits the 1928 enthronement and the IEEJ honour. It says Niwa directed a University of Tokyo department; IEEJ says he became the first president of Tokyo Denki University. There is no IEEE milestone for this machine. The IEEE milestone is for G3 in 1980.

**Numbers**
- **Page times.** Wikipedia puts G3 at 6–15 s or 40 s per page. The IEEE milestone and ETHW call G3 the "one-minute facsimile". Coopersmith (TAMU) and the Sharp FO-2000 spec give about 20 s by the early 1980s. The figures measure different things (with or without handshake; MH vs MR; 4800 vs 9600 bit/s).
- **US fax numbers** mix installed base and annual sales and conflict between sources: 4 million installed by 1989 (EBSCO) vs 5 million by 1990 (ETHW); 1987 sales of 295,000 (CAP), 424,600 (NOPA), or 500,000 (Dataquest).
- **Finch home receiver.** Mannes: $79.50 ($49.95 kit), 11 min per page. Schneider: $125, about 20 min per 12-inch page. They may describe different models or years.
- **NHS fax count.** Wikipedia cites Labour's 11,620 (2018). RCS reported 8,946 and the government "more than 8,000". The deadline is "31 March 2020" in the press release and "April 2020" in its summary.
- **Magnafax weight.** 46 lb per Wikipedia and BizTech; one secondary source in search results says 37 lb (unverified).

**Not in Wikipedia**
- **Ultrafax**, which Wikipedia barely covers: a 1,047-page novel in about 2 minutes, "a million words a minute", film-based, with Kodak and NBC.
- **Western Union Telecar**, which Wikipedia mentions in one line: the 1947 Baltimore experiment, FCC frequency fight, 65-second messages, 3 cars per channel, and a 7-car city fleet by 1951.
- **Japan's role in the standard.** Wikipedia credits "Japanese firms". The milestone adds NTT/KDD's Modified READ, the free patent pledge at the CCITT Kyoto meeting, MPT leadership, and NTT F-Net (1981). Musmann adds that MH itself was a compromise among IBM, KDD, and the German PTT.
- **Why Japan, beyond "preference for handwriting".** Coopersmith adds kanji (no telex alternative), non-sequential street addresses (fax maps), hanko seals, the 1972 opening of the phone network, and the early-1970s "information society" policy. No source I read names MITI.
- **Fax as a political tool.** Tiananmen 1989 and the 1991 coup are absent from Wikipedia. CSM and UPI give names, counts, and timestamps.
- **2020s healthcare.** Wikipedia stops at 2018–19. ONC data show 67% of US hospitals still used fax in 2023, and NHS trust FOIs by 2025–26 mostly report zero machines.
