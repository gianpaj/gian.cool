# Storylines: the history of the fax machine (about 5 min)

The one idea: a fax cuts a picture into lines of dots and keeps two machines in step. Inventors solved that again and again for 180 years, and going digital made it fast.

Every fact comes from `../../notes/`, `../../technology/` and `../../video/fact-check.md`. Images and sounds come from `../../images/` and `../../audio/`.

## A. One page, 180 years (recommended)

**Device:** a worked example. One handwritten page goes through every fax machine in history.
**Form:** an object-to-object chain. Each machine's copy becomes the next machine's original.

The viewer watches the same page get scanned by Bain's pendulum, Caselli's pantelegraph, a Belin photo drum, a radiofax recorder, a 1980 Group 3 machine and a T.38 gateway. It comes out each time as that era's real copy: sheared lines, blue pantelegram ink, photo greys, a weather chart, thermal paper, packets. The viewer feels the same problem being solved again and again until the bits win.

**First two seconds:** a thermal page curls out of a 1990s fax at full frame while the real 2,100 Hz answer tone plays. One line on screen: "Why does a fax scream?"

**Beats:**
1. Hook.
2. 1843 Bain: the page cut into lines; the pendulums drift and the copy shears.
3. 1865 Caselli: a real Paris–Lyon pantelegram.
4. 1902–1924 photo-telegraphy: light becomes current; Bartlane's 5 grey levels are the first digital island.
5. Radio: weather charts; the radio-newspaper flop.
6. 1960s–70s analog office fax: 6 minutes a page.
7. 1980 Group 3: the page as 1,728 bits per line; the run-length example (67 pixels to 33 bits); Japan offers its patents free.
8. Payoff: the scream decoded, tone by tone.
9. The 1990s boom and the Tiananmen faxes.
10. Today: T.38 and hospitals.
11. Closing line.

**Look:** every image arrives through a real 1-bit fax treatment (1,728 dots across, 98 lines per inch), in black and fax-white. One signal-orange accent is used only on the moving scan line, which carries the eye through every era.

**Sound:** narrated, so the voice sets the clock. A sparse clockwork pulse plays through the mechanical eras and opens into a warmer bed at 1980. The real tone, Hellschreiber and radiofax recordings play as effects. The music drops to silence for the scream decode.

## A′. One page, 180 years, with a twist: the first fax was digital

Same page, same chain as A. It's told as an argument instead of a chronology.

**The twist:** fax started digital, with on/off dots in 1843. It went analog for a century to carry greys, and came back to digital in 1980.

**What the viewer remembers:** a picture is just dots sent in step.

**Wording caveats** (`../fact-check.md`):
- On screen, use "on/off dots" rather than "bits"; nobody in 1843 called them bits.
- Give the compression ratio as a range.
- Say "across the Atlantic" for the Coolidge photo, because the direction is disputed.

## B. Decode the scream

**Device:** the object is the explanation. It is the real 10-second fax handshake recording.
**Form:** an endless zoom into its spectrogram. Each signal opens onto the history behind it, going backwards in time.

The recording plays once, then stops on each sound:
- The 2,100 Hz tone leads into the phone network and its echo suppressors.
- The 300 bit/s warble leads into modems, the 1980 standard and Japan.
- The page hiss leads into run-length compression.
- A single scan line leads back to Caselli and Bain in 1843.

At the end the whole recording plays again, and the viewer can now read it.

**First two seconds:** a full-frame spectrogram scrolls with the 2,100 Hz tone at full volume. One line: "This is a fax saying hello."

**Beats:**
1. Hook.
2. The whole 10 s recording.
3. The 2,100 Hz tone: "I'm a fax".
4. The FSK warble: two machines negotiating.
5. Why a standard: 1980, kanji, free patents.
6. The hiss: the page as runs.
7. The scan line: Bain and Caselli, and the sync problem.
8. Zoom back out: play the recording again and read along.
9. Today.
10. Closing line.

**Look:** the spectrogram is printed like a weather-fax chart, near-black on wet electrolytic grey paper. The 2,100 Hz line is the one accent.

**Sound:** the recording is the score. A minimal bed sits under the narration, with silence before each tone.

## C. Only as good as the other fax

**Device:** a number lands, as a network story.
**Form:** a flight across one map that fills with lines and then empties.

A machine invented in 1843 took 140 years to matter, because a fax is worthless until the other side has one. Lines draw across the map:
- Paris–Lyon, 1865.
- New York–London, 1924.
- Tokyo, 1928.
- Beijing, 1989.
- Moscow, 1991.

Then the US count climbs from 25,000 machines (1970) to 5 million (1990), and the lines go dark as email arrives. Hospitals and Japan stay lit.

**First two seconds:** one fax machine sits alone on an engraved map, dialing a line that leads nowhere. One line: "The first fax had no one to call."

**Beats:**
1. Hook.
2. 1843: no one to send to.
3. 1865: Paris–Lyon, too costly.
4. Newspapers and police pay for it.
5. The radio-newspaper flop.
6. 1960s: 100× the cost of a letter.
7. 1972: Japan opens its phone lines.
8. 1980: any brand to any brand.
9. 25,000 to 5 million.
10. Tiananmen and the coup faxes.
11. Email.
12. Who still faxes.
13. Closing line.

**Look:** the real 1901 Eastern Telegraph cable map as the ground, with routes in one ink colour.

**Sound:** the music builds as the network fills and thins at the end. Dial and handshake recordings mark each new connection.
