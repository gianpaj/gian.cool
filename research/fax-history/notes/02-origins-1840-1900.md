# Era 1: copying telegraphs (1843–1900)

Fax is older than the telephone. It grew out of the electric telegraph and clockmaking, and its central problem from day one was **synchronisation**: the sending and receiving machines must scan the same spot at the same moment, or the picture shears apart.

## Alexander Bain (1843)

- Scottish clockmaker from Caithness. He also patented the electric clock and fought Charles Wheatstone over credit for it.
- 27 May 1843: British patent for "improvements in producing and regulating electric currents and improvements in timepieces, and in electric printing, and signal telegraphs".
- How it worked: the message is set in raised metal type (or pins) on an insulating cylinder. A probe on a clock-driven pendulum sweeps across it line by line. Where it touches metal, current flows; where it doesn't, nothing. At the receiver a matching pendulum drags a stylus over paper soaked in chemicals, which darkens wherever current flows.
- Bain's patent claim: "a copy of any other surface composed of conducting and non-conducting materials can be taken by these means".
- Five wires joined transmitter and receiver. It stayed a lab device: the two ends never truly stayed in sync.
- Story angle: Bain's pixels were **on or off**, contact or no contact, so the first fax was effectively a 1-bit image. Fax returned to 1-bit images in 1980 with Group 3.
- Bain was posthumously awarded a Technology & Engineering Emmy in 2016 "for his pioneering work in the transmission of images".
- Patent dates: the British patent is dated 27 May 1843 and its specification was enrolled on 27 November. His US patent 5,957 (5 Dec 1848) describes sending "copies of surfaces" between Boston and New York.
- Coopersmith frames fax history as a run of rivalries: Bain vs Bakewell (1840s), Caselli vs Meyer (1860s), Korn vs Belin (1910s), Wirephoto vs Soundphoto (1930s), Western Union vs Xerox (1950s–60s). Promoters kept promising "This time it will be different."

## Frederick Bakewell (1847–48)

- English physicist. Replaced Bain's pendulums with **rotating cylinders** driven by clockwork, with a stylus advanced by a screw thread. That is the drum-and-lead-screw layout every analog fax used for the next 120 years.
- Original written in insulating ink on metal foil. Each time the stylus crossed ink, the current broke.
- Shown at the Great Exhibition of 1851. Could send handwriting and simple drawings; never commercial. Same sync problem.

## Giovanni Caselli and the pantelegraph (1856–1870)

- Italian priest and physics professor (Florence). Took part in the 1848 insurrection in Parma and was expelled.
- Name: *pan* (all) + *telegraph*, echoing the pantograph copying instrument.
- Key innovation: a **regulating clock** with an 8 kg pendulum on a 2 m frame that kept both ends in step. Two messages were scanned per pendulum swing, one each way.
- Original: non-conductive ink on tin foil. Receiver: paper soaked in potassium ferricyanide, which turns blue under current.
- Speed: a 111 × 27 mm slip with about 25 handwritten words took **108 seconds**.
- Milestones: Napoleon III became a backer in 1860; Paris–Amiens test sent Rossini's signature (Rossini wrote a piano piece about it); first pantelegram Lyon→Paris on 10 Feb 1862; commercial Paris–Lyon service from 1865, extended to Marseille in 1867. Price: 20 centimes per cm².
- Main use: **signature verification for banks**. About 5,000 faxes in the first year, peaking at 110 an hour.
- The Musée des arts et métiers describes the 1861 machine as two chronometers synchronised by electromagnets. It also says the 20-centime tariff made the service "prohibitively expensive", that only two lines were built, and that Caselli's obituary called the pantelegraph "a museum piece". A Paris–Le Havre pantelegram from about 1867 survives.
- Ended in 1870 with the Franco-Prussian War. Survivors: Musée des Arts et Métiers (Paris), Museo Galileo (Florence), Popov Museum (St Petersburg), a 1933 replica in Milan. In 1982 two pantelegraphs at the Riquewihr Postal Museum ran six hours a day for months without error.
- Story angle: a working fax network ran **11 years before the telephone existed**.

## Shelford Bidwell (1880–81)

- English barrister turned physicist. Put a **selenium photocell** inside a rotating cylinder, so the machine read light and dark rather than conductive and non-conductive. It could scan any flat image, not just specially prepared originals.
- Reported as "Tele-Photography" in Nature, 10 Feb 1881. The device is in the Science Museum, London.
- Sync was still unsolved: both cylinders sat on one shaft.
- In 1908 he estimated that "electric vision" (TV) would need 16,000–150,000 picture elements refreshed 10 times a second. That bandwidth problem is the same one fax faced at a slower pace.

## Elisha Gray's telautograph (1888)

- Gray, Bell's rival in the telephone race, patented the telautograph on 31 July 1888: "one to transmit his own handwriting to a distant point over a two-wire circuit".
- It does not scan. It sends the **position of the pen**, and a pen at the far end follows it.
- **The digital detour.** Gray tried analog first, was dissatisfied, and switched to **pulses**: each pulse meant one small step of pen movement, polarity gave direction, and current interruptions meant pen-up and paper-feed. Fast handwriting needed roughly 600–1,000 pulses per second, too much for electromechanics on telegraph lines. Around 1900 Foster Ritchie went back to **analog** (rheostats and a dither AC signal for pen-down), and analog telautographs ran for decades.
- Used by banks, hospitals and railway stations; Grand Central Terminal had a public telautograph display into the 1960s. In 1911 one warned workers on the 10th floor of the Triangle Shirtwaist Factory about the fire.
- The company became part of Xerox in 1999 (Omnifax).
- Story angle: digital was the right idea in 1888 but the hardware couldn't keep up. That pattern repeats until the 1970s.

## Others worth a line

- **Bell's "autograph telegraph"** (US patent 161,739, April 1875).
- **Ernest Hummel's Telediagraph** (1895): shellac-on-foil originals over a dedicated circuit between five big US newspapers. The commercial ancestor of wirephoto.
- **Amstutz Electro-Artograph** (1895, Scientific American): engraved-relief picture transmission.
- **Henry Sutton's "telephane"** (account published 1896).
- **Paul Nipkow's disc** (1884): a spinning disc with a spiral of holes that scans an image line by line. Fax scanned the same way, only slower.
