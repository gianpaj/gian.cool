# Era 4: the office fax boom (1960–2000)

Fax took about 140 years to become ordinary. Until the 1960s a fax cost about 100 times more than a letter (Coopersmith). Four things finally lined up: legal access to the phone network, international standards so any two machines could talk, digital compression that cut a page from minutes to seconds, and Japanese demand and manufacturing. The digital switch is the turning point of the whole story.

## Analog office fax (1964–1980)

- **Xerox LDX** ("Long Distance Xerography", announced 5 May 1964): a cathode-ray flying-spot scanner and a xerographic printer, about 1,100 lb, 8 pages a minute. It needed microwave, coax or special lines and rented for $800 a month. Often called the first commercial modern fax.
- **Xerox Magnafax Telecopier** (1966): 46 lb, plugged into any phone line, a letter page in about **6 minutes**, rented from about $50 a month. The *Life* ad said "Mail letters over the phone!" It set the template for the next decade.
- **Exxon Qwip** (1970s–80s): a drum transceiver. Operators made a voice call first, then put the handset on the machine, which read the page line by line, "converting image into sound" (The Henry Ford museum).
- How analog fax worked: the page spins on a drum; reflected light hits a photocell; the current drives a tone generator whose **frequency** follows brightness; the tone goes into the handset through an acoustic coupler. The receiver turns tone back into current and moves a stylus over paper on an identical drum spinning at the same rate.
- In the 1960s a page took about **15 minutes**, and one page from Germany to the US cost 20–30 Deutschmarks (Hans Musmann, IEEE oral history). Makers built **deliberately incompatible** machines to lock customers in.

## The legal unlock

- **US, 1968:** before Carterfone, AT&T could bar any device it didn't own from being wired to its network, which is why early couplers worked through sound. The FCC's Carterfone decision (13 F.C.C.2d 420) allowed "any lawful device" that didn't harm the network. US deregulation then proceeded gradually.
- **Japan, 1972:** a revision of the Public Telecommunications Act opened NTT's phone lines to fax in one step (Coopersmith; Ricoh).

## Standards: Group 1 → Group 4

| Group | Standard | Year | Signal | Time per page | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| G1 | CCITT T.2 | 1968 | Analog (FM tones) | ~6 min | Little used |
| G2 | CCITT T.3 | 1976 | Analog | ~3 min | "Three-minute facsimile"; first real interoperability |
| G3 | CCITT T.4 + T.30 | 1980 | **Digital**, Modified Huffman (MR optional) | "one-minute facsimile", ~20 s by the early 1980s, 6–15 s later | 204 × 98 dpi "standard", 204 × 196 "fine"; 2,400–14,400 bit/s; V.34 (33.6 kbit/s, "Super G3") from 1994 |
| G4 | CCITT T.6 etc. | 1984 | Digital over ISDN (64 kbit/s) | seconds | MMR compression; never common outside ISDN markets |

Years and labels are confirmed by the IEEE milestone text and Coopersmith. G1/G2 were withdrawn as obsolete in July 1996. Group 3 is still the fax people mean today.

## Who made fax digital?

- **Dacom (US).** Filed a run-length compression patent in 1969 (US 4,135,214, for "business letters, weather maps, newspapers"), building on Lockheed's satellite-image coding. Wikipedia calls the Dacom "Rapidfax" the first digital fax; the product name is **Rapifax**.
- **SRI + Ricoh.** In 1970 SRI was commissioned by Savin, CBS and Dacom to build a digital fax; its prototype ran at 4,800 bit/s. Ricoh licensed the work and launched the **RIFAX 600S** in 1973: an A4 page in about a minute, against up to 6 minutes for analog. It was demonstrated Tokyo–New York by satellite in April 1973 and sold in the US as Rapifax 100 from 1974. SRI and Ricoh call it the first commercial digital fax. ⚠ The two "first" claims are not reconciled.
- **The CCITT standard (1976–80).** Competing compression proposals came from IBM, Japan's KDD and the German PTT with TU Braunschweig. "The result was a compromise, called a modified Huffman code," which cut the bits about 4× (Musmann). Japan's NTT and KDD developed **Modified READ**, a 2D code with about twice MH's compression. At the CCITT Kyoto meeting the Japanese delegation offered the MR patents **free of charge** if MR was adopted. The IEEE milestone "International Standardization of G3 Facsimile, 1980" (dedicated 2012) commemorates this.
- Musmann's verdict: "facsimile became the first digital transmission of visual information, I think."

## Why Japan drove the boom

- **Language.** Kanji has thousands of characters and typewriters and telex handled it badly, so handwriting by fax was the natural business tool. "There was a special need for Japan; the Western countries already had Telex" (Musmann).
- **Addresses.** Japanese street addresses aren't sequential, so people faxed hand-drawn maps to visitors (Coopersmith, p. 168).
- **Hanko** seals on documents.
- **Policy.** Deregulation in 1972. The early-1970s "information society" (jōhōka shakai) vision folded fax into national planning. The Ministry of Posts and Telecommunications, NTT and KDD coordinated a domestic standard and carried it into G3. No source found names MITI.
- **Supply.** Consumer-electronics makers competed fiercely while cooperating on compatibility. Sharp's FO-2000 "HAYAX" (1981) sent an A4 page in 20 s at 9,600 bit/s, weighed 70 kg and cost about ¥3 million. NTT opened its F-Net fax network the same year.
- Result: by 1990 the US had about 5 million fax machines, "nearly all made in Japan".

Sources: Coopersmith (ETHW), Christian Science Monitor 1987/1989 citing Dataquest, NOPA and CAP International, NYT 2005. Installed-base and sales figures shouldn't be mixed on one chart. Japan's stand-alone sales peaked in 2000.

- Prices: under $1,000 by 1989, against $7,500–10,000 a decade earlier. A 20-page document cost $1.25 by fax and $14 by FedEx. FedEx's own satellite fax service, **ZapMail** (1984–86), lost over $300 million.
- The late-1980s craze: restaurants took lunch orders by fax; people faxed prescriptions, homework and valentines; Tokyo coffee shops had coin-op faxes. A public-fax directory grew from 411 entries (Aug 1986) to 9,000 (Jan 1989). "Within three months, we had forgotten what it was like before we had it" (Tim Choy, PR partner, CSM 1989).
- Typical 1980s–90s units used **direct thermal printing** on rolls of heat-sensitive paper: curly, fading, and refused as evidence by some courts and archives unless photocopied. Plain-paper fax (thermal transfer, inkjet, laser) took over from the mid-1990s.
- Telex declined as fax rose in the 1980s.

## Fax and politics

- **Tiananmen, June 1989.** About 20 Caltech students faxed Western news reports to businesses in China ("We can fax back pictures and that is probably more convincing than just oral reports"). The Chinese Alliance for Democracy ran 10 fax machines in the US, Tokyo, Hong Kong and South America. Chinese authorities "started taking away fax machines" (Christian Science Monitor).
- **Soviet coup, August 1991.** The first alert to reach Washington was a fax from a Yeltsin-government official, timed 10:54 p.m. EDT on Sunday 18 August: "It is Military Coup. Tanks are everywhere." It came before the first news reports. "They tried to shut down the faxes, but we're still getting them" (Allen Weinstein, Center for Democracy; UPI).
- Coopersmith's 2017 talk "Forging the Fax" treats fax-borne junk and misinformation as a preview of spam and "alternative facts".

## Fax meets the PC

- 1985 ⚠: Hank Magnuski's GammaLink ships **GammaFax**, the first PC fax board. GammaLink merged into Dialogic in 1994.
- Early 1990s: small offices run a fax/modem card with WinFax Pro. The 1992 SupraFAXModem 14400 at $399 set off a price war.
- Fax classes describe how the work is split between modem and PC: Class 1 (T.31; the PC does compression and T.30), "Class 2" (vendor drafts), Class 2.0 (T.32), Class 2.1 (V.34).
- About 1989 Paul Baran (packet-switching pioneer) co-founded Interfax, whose computers read incoming faxes.
- The same cheap fax boards enabled **junk fax**. The US Telephone Consumer Protection Act (1991) banned unsolicited fax ads, partly because the recipient paid for the paper.

## Fax as art (1980–1990)

- 31 Oct 1980: Paulo Bruscky (Recife) and Roberto Sandoval (São Paulo) make Brazil's first artistic fax transmission.
- 12 Jan 1985: Joseph Beuys, Andy Warhol and Kaii Higashiyama's "Global-Art-Fusion" fax travels Düsseldorf → New York → Tokyo and arrives in Vienna within 32 minutes, framed as a statement of peace during the Cold War.
