# Era 3: radio fax, newspapers in the living room, war and weather (1920–1970)

Once a picture can travel as a varying tone, any channel that carries sound can carry it, radio included. This era produced the first attempt at fax for the home, which failed, and fax's longest-running niche: weather maps for ships.

## Ranger's photoradiogram (1924–26)

- Richard H. Ranger (RCA; ex-Army Signal Corps; MIT) invented the "wireless photoradiogram".
- Late November 1924: a photo of President Calvin Coolidge crossed the Atlantic by radio, the first transoceanic radio photo. Commercial service from 1926. ⚠ Wikipedia says New York → London on 29 Nov; *Radio News* (Feb 1925) describes a public demo on 30 Nov from London (Marconi House) via Carnarvon and Riverhead to Broad Street, New York. Tests probably ran both ways; say "across the Atlantic" until a primary RCA or NYT account settles it.
- Ranger's 1925 patent (US 1,656,338) records shades of grey as **variably spaced dots**: an on/off picture code, 55 years before Group 3.
- Ranger later built the NBC chime machine, studied captured German tape recorders after WWII, and won an Oscar (1956) for film-sound sync.

## Jenkins's radio-photo letters (1924)

- C. Francis Jenkins sent typed letters by radio at about 100 words a minute. Letters to Senator R. B. Howell (October 1924) argued that photo copies of letters were "admissible in court", an early version of fax's legal-signature appeal (*Radio News*, Feb 1925).

## Fax for the home, take one: Fultograph (1928–32)

- Otho Fulton's clockwork receiver plugged into a radio's loudspeaker socket. A stylus darkened paper soaked in potassium iodide and starch, wrapped on a drum. It looked like a cylinder phonograph.
- Price £22 15s in 1928, and it needed a good radio. The BBC broadcast Fultograph pictures in 759 programmes (1929–32), about 10 images a day.
- Side uses: a modified version recorded ASDIC sonar echoes; Lufthansa radioed weather charts to aircraft in flight (Oct 1929).

## Fax for the home, take two: the radio newspaper (1933–1950s)

- 1931: GE (Charles J. Young, Ernst Alexanderson) faxed the Schenectady Union-Star to two transatlantic liners; 15 minutes per page.
- 1933: William Finch tests his Finch Facsimile system in New Jersey. 1937: first radio-fax newspaper in Minneapolis–St Paul. 1938: WOR New York broadcasts a "wireless newspaper". 1939: W9XZY St. Louis delivers a daily paper by radio fax; over 1,000 homes get receivers that print overnight on thermal paper.
- William G. H. Finch: born in Birmingham, England, in 1897; engineer for Hearst's International News Service; briefly assistant chief engineer at the FCC; founded his company in 1935; more than 200 patents.
- The hardware: a home recorder cost $79.50 ($49.95 as a kit) in one account and $125 in another. It printed on a 5-inch thermal roll (about $1 a week), took 11–20 minutes per page, and kept in sync with the 60 Hz mains. Crosley sold a version as the "Reado" from 1939.
- The rollout: FCC experimental licences in 1937–38 (KSTP, WHO, WGH, WOR, WGN, WHK, WSM, WWJ). RCA ran an overnight service on KSD and then UHF station W9XZY. The St. Louis Post-Dispatch "Radio Edition" started on 7 Dec 1938 with 15 receivers, and McClatchy's "Radio Bee" bought 100. More than 20 US stations tried fax broadcasting in the 1930s–40s.
- It failed. Receivers were expensive and jammed, static garbled pages, there were two incompatible standards, and advertisers stayed away. The public "had neither asked for, nor cared about, the technology" (*Radio World*). WWII paper shortages ended most stations. A post-war revival (the Miami Herald's fax edition for about a year from 1948; New York Times fax bulletins; WGHF tests on a subcarrier in 1945) faded, and Finch's company was bankrupt by 1952.
- Story angle: the "push news to a printer at home" dream came 60 years too early. The web eventually delivered it.

## Hellschreiber (1929): a fax for letters

- Rudolf Hell (Munich; later founded Hell in Kiel, merged into Linotype-Hell) patented it in 1929.
- Each character is sent as columns of a **7 × 7 pixel grid**, on/off, at 112.5 baud. A spinning helix presses dots into paper tape. Every column prints twice, so timing drift tilts the text instead of destroying it.
- Readable over terrible radio links, where human eyes do the error correction. Used by German forces in WWII (sometimes with Enigma) and by newswires into the 1980s; ham operators still use it ("Feld-Hell").
- Story angle: Hellschreiber is a **bitmap font sent as a fax**, a link between fax and digital text. Listen to `../audio/hellschreiber-signal.ogg`.

## War pictures (1941–45)

- Fax carried maps, orders and weather charts. AP, UPI and Reuters ran radio-fax transmitters close to the front.
- The Iwo Jima flag-raising photo was radio-faxed from Guam to New York (12,781 km) and printed in hundreds of US papers within a day.
- US forces radio-faxed proposed leaflet designs from Honolulu to the Saipan press.

## Post-war: Desk-Fax, Telecar, Ultrafax, Muirhead

- **Desk-Fax (1948).** Western Union engineer Garvice Ridings designed a small, cheap desktop fax for sending and receiving telegrams, printed on spark-printer paper. It had prototypes in 8 cities in 1949 and "over 40,000 machines" in the 1950s. No phone-line fax matched that volume until the 1970s (Coopersmith).
- **Telecar (1947–51).** Western Union's 1947 Baltimore experiment put a fax recorder and two-way radio in a 1946 Plymouth, so telegrams printed in the van on the way to the customer. Each message took about 65 s, so one channel could serve only 3 cars. About 4% of messages had to be re-sent. The FCC moved the service from 152–162 MHz to 35–44 MHz. By 1951 Baltimore had 7 Telecars (*Western Union Technical Review*, Apr 1951).
- **Ultrafax (1948).** RCA, with Kodak and NBC, demonstrated "a million words a minute" at the Library of Congress in October 1948. A cathode-ray flying spot scanned microfilm and the receiver recorded onto film. *Gone with the Wind*, all 1,047 pages, went in about two minutes over a 3-mile TV link. Sarnoff compared it to splitting the atom. It never became a service.
- **Muirhead & Co.** (UK) built photo transmitters (journalists used them at the 1952 Oslo Olympics) and later the Mufax line.

## Weatherfax: the niche that never died

- The US National Weather Service started weatherfax in the 1950s: land lines first, then HF radio to ships.
- Still broadcast today from US Coast Guard stations (NMG New Orleans, NMC Point Reyes, NMF Boston, NOJ Kodiak), Germany's DWD (Pinneberg), Japan, Australia, Chile and the UK (Northwood). Kyodo News still radio-faxes whole newspapers to fishing fleets at 60 lines per minute (half speed, because kanji are dense).
- How it sounds: single sideband, black at 1,500 Hz and white at 2,300 Hz, greys in between; usually 120 lines per minute; "index of cooperation" 576. A start tone spins up the receiver drum and a phasing signal centres the image. These are the same sync problems Bain had, solved with tones.

## Space and slow-scan TV

- 1960s: the US Army sent the first satellite fax photo (Courier satellite, to Puerto Rico).
- 1966: the Soviet Luna 9 sent the first pictures from the Moon's surface in a radiofax-compatible format. Jodrell Bank decoded them with a borrowed newspaper fax receiver and the Daily Express published them before the Soviets did.
- Slow-scan TV (proposed 1957–58) is radiofax's cousin: FM tones, about 120 lines, fits a 3 kHz voice channel. NASA used it on Mercury (Gordon Cooper, 1963).
